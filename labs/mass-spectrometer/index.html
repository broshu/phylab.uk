Mass Spectrometer: how it works

① Chamber A: ionisation

The gas in chamber A is ionised, producing positive ions that drift through the small hole S1 into the accelerating field.

The initial speed is tiny, so all the kinetic energy comes from the work done by the electric force. This is the starting point of the next step.

② Accelerating field: work–energy theorem

The voltage between plates M (+) and N (−) is U, and the electric force does positive work:

The larger U is, the larger v is; for the same voltage, the larger the mass, the smaller the speed.

③ Velocity selector: two forces balance

The electric field E points right, the magnetic field B1 points out of the page, and the ions travel vertically downward. They go straight through only when the electric force equals the Lorentz force:

If the speed is too large the Lorentz force wins and the ion deflects left; if it is too small the electric force wins and it deflects right. In the demonstration E is tuned automatically with U so the beam always passes.

④ Deflecting field: circular motion

The ions enter B0 from S3 with the same speed v0, and the Lorentz force alone provides the centripetal force:

Since v0 is the same, r ∝ m: the larger the mass, the larger the radius.

⑤ Photographic plate: landing point

The ion turns through half a circle and lands on the plate, on the same horizontal line as S3:

X is the diameter of the semicircle; the mass can be worked out from the landing point.

Conclusion: why add a velocity selector?

① The selector makes every ion enter B0 with the same speed, so X ∝ m.

② The landing points of the three hydrogen isotopes are in the ratio

Equal mass differences give equally spaced lines, which makes the mass easy to read (without the selector X ∝ √m).

③ Measuring X gives the mass. This is how a mass spectrometer determines isotope masses; typical uses include separating the isotopes of hydrogen and discovering the isotopes of neon.
