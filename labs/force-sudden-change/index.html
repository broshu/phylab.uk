Key ideas

1Rigid objects: the elastic force can change suddenly

Strings, rods, wires and table tops obey F = kx just like springs, but with a very large k: a steel wire 1 m long and 1 mm in diameter has k ≈ 1.6×10⁵ N/m. A 30 N change in force needs the wire to stretch or shrink by only about 0.2 mm, and the ball covers that tiny distance in a few milliseconds. Compared with the process we are studying (a fraction of a second) this is negligible, so we say the force “can change suddenly”.

2Springs: the elastic force cannot change suddenly

A spring has a small k (50 N/m here), so changing the force by 30 N means stretching or compressing it by 60 cm. The ends of a spring are attached to objects with mass, and moving them 60 cm takes time. So at the instant another force changes suddenly, the spring’s deformation has no time to change, its force stays the same, and the acceleration of the object jumps. If the spring itself is cut, or one end is attached to nothing, its force vanishes immediately.

3Friction: three kinds of sudden change

Static friction comes from tiny elastic deformations of the contact points, so like a rigid object it can change suddenly with the pull (in both size and direction). When the pull exceeds the maximum static friction, the contact points break in bulk, the object slides, and the friction jumps to the smaller sliding friction (static → sliding). When the object stops, it jumps back to static friction (sliding → static).

The microscopic picture

Elastic force comes from tiny changes in the spacing between atoms. In a rigid solid a change of only a few parts per thousand gives a large force, and the change spreads at the speed of sound (about 5×10³ m/s in steel), so it crosses a 1 m wire in about 0.2 ms. In the wire of a spring the deformation between atoms is just as small; it is the coiled shape that magnifies it into an extension of tens of centimetres. Two rubbing surfaces touch only at a few bumps. While sliding, the contact points keep breaking and re-forming with uneven deformation, so sliding friction is smaller than the maximum static friction.

What “suddenly” means, and the models used

“Suddenly” means the force changes in a time much shorter than the process being studied. It does not mean it takes no time: slow the playback to ×1/1000 and the elastic force of a rigid object also changes continuously. The model uses g = 10 m/s², a 1 kg ball, a maximum static friction of 5 N and a sliding friction of 3.5 N (10 : 7). The atomic chain and the contact points are one-dimensional sketches whose orders of magnitude are estimated for iron and ordinary surfaces.
